Relationships Across Generations and Groups

Part C of Understanding the Nature and Dynamics of Social Relationships, with the lesson-wide retrieval check and synthesis. This is not graded. In Facilitated mode, notes appear before group activities; in Self-Paced mode, work through independently.

Learn

Intergenerational solidarity

Bengtson and Roberts describe several dimensions of closeness in families. A family can be strong in one and strained in another (Bengtson & Roberts, 1991).

DimensionMeaningExample
AssociationalContact and shared activity.A regular meal with grandparents.
AffectualEmotional warmth and closeness.Feeling able to share a worry.
ConsensualAgreement in values or opinions.Similar views on a career or belief.
FunctionalGiving and receiving assistance.Help with tuition or using a phone.
NormativeObligations and family roles.A sense of responsibility to care for elders.
StructuralOpportunities for contact.Living together or living far apart.
Living together does not by itself guarantee emotional closeness or agreement.

Practice

Practice 9: intergenerational solidarity

A. You live far from your grandparents, which limits opportunities to meet. Which dimension describes this constraint?
B. Your parent helps with tuition. You help your grandparent use a phone. Which dimension describes these exchanges?
C. You feel you must care for your lola because she raised you.
D. You share the same political and career views as your parents.

E. Can family members feel emotionally close while disagreeing about a career? Name the two dimensions involved.

See the answer key

A. D: distance and living arrangements affect opportunities for contact. Actual contact frequency can be different and falls under associational solidarity.

B. A: this dimension concerns giving and receiving practical help.

C. C: obligations and family roles are normative solidarity.

D. B: agreement in values and opinions is consensual solidarity.

E. Yes. Affectual solidarity concerns emotional closeness. Consensual solidarity concerns agreement on values or opinions. One can be high while the other is lower.

Learn

Closeness and tension across generations

Intergenerational ambivalence. Warmth and tension can coexist. You can love a grandparent and disagree about your future (Luscher & Pillemer, 1998).

Respeto with a voice. Respectful disagreement can acknowledge the relationship while clearly stating a need. Gestures such as mano po express respect, and respect does not require silence.

Generational labels. "Gen Z" or "boomer" can hide differences in personality, age, experience, and access. Ask about the actual person. Meta-analytic evidence on generational differences in work-related traits is small and inconsistent (Costanza et al., 2012).

Evidence caution. Evidence on structured intergenerational programs is limited or inconclusive. Benefits should not be assumed for every program.

Sources: Luscher & Pillemer (1998); Costanza et al. (2012); Ignatius & Hechanova (2014).

Practice

Practice 10: generational relationships

A. You love your parent and value their support, but feel frustrated by a rule limiting your independence. Which concept describes the mixed feelings?
B. A classmate says, "All older people resist change." What is the problem with this explanation?
C. Which sentence best shows respect with a voice?

D. Draft one sentence that acknowledges an older person's concern while expressing your own plan.

See the answer key

A. B: closeness and tension can coexist in the same relationship. The feelings do not establish that the relationship is dysfunctional.

B. C: a broad label overlooks individual experience, age, context, and access. Ask about the actual situation.

C. A: it acknowledges the concern, states a plan, and invites discussion.

D. One possible sentence: "I understand why safety matters to you. Could I explain my plan and discuss a way to address your concern?"

Pause C1: studying far from home

Facilitator noteUse as a silent-writing round of three minutes, then invite volunteers to read their response aloud without commentary on whose family is right.
Scenario. Your grandmother says studying far from home is not what a good grandchild does. You love her and you want the opportunity.
  1. Which solidarity dimensions and tensions are relevant?
  2. Write one response that expresses respect and your own plan.
See feedback

Dimensions involved: normative solidarity concerns family duty. Consensual solidarity concerns disagreement over values. Distance may change structural opportunities for contact.

Ambivalence: love and guilt can coexist with a desire for independence. The emotions do not require anyone to be the villain.

Possible response: "I know staying close matters to you, and you matter to me. I want to take this opportunity, and I would like us to plan how we will stay connected."

Learn

Intergroup relationships and social identity

Intergroup relationships. People relate as members of groups such as sections, strands, regions, faiths, or fandoms.

Social identity. Part of our sense of self comes from belonging to groups. That can support pride and belonging (Tajfel & Turner, 1979).

In-group favoritism. People can favor their own group even when group divisions are arbitrary, as the minimal-group studies illustrate.

The risk. A group label can replace curiosity about the individual. Notice when "they are all like that" ends the conversation.

Kapwa offers a Filipino lens: relating to others as sharing a common humanity (hindi ibang-tao) rather than as outsiders (ibang-tao).

Learn

Shared goals and the contact hypothesis

Robbers Cave. Competition between camp groups created hostility. Shared goals that required both groups' cooperation helped reduce it. The study has limitations, so treat it as an illustration, not a universal rule (Sherif et al., 1961).

Superordinate goal. A goal that neither group can achieve alone. Every group's contribution becomes necessary.

Contact hypothesis. Intergroup contact often relates to reduced prejudice. Favorable conditions can strengthen it, but results depend on the context (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Simply putting groups in one space does not guarantee better relations.

Allport's favorable contact conditions

ConditionDesign choice at school
Equal statusGive mixed-group members meaningful roles and equal voice.
Common goalsChoose one outcome everyone needs.
CooperationDesign complementary tasks that require joint effort.
Authority supportTeachers or leaders support inclusion and fair participation.
Pettigrew and Tropp found that these conditions enhance contact effects rather than being absolute prerequisites.

Practice

Practice 11: contact across groups

A. Mixed-section teams give every student a meaningful role and a chance to contribute. Which contact condition does this support?
B. Two sections must combine their resources to complete one service project that neither can finish alone.
C. Would placing rival sections in the same room automatically improve their relationship?
D. The principal publicly states that the mixed-section event is fair and that all students will be heard.
See the answer key

A. B: meaningful roles and equal voice support equal status within the activity.

B. D: neither group can achieve it alone. The design supports common goals and cooperation. Teacher support can strengthen fair participation.

C. C: shared work, equal footing, and supportive leaders make contact constructive.

D. A: leaders who back inclusion and fair participation provide authority support.

Practice

Pause C2: an inter-section event

Facilitator noteHave each group redesign the event on a half sheet. Count aloud how many Allport conditions each design includes.
Scenario. Two sections have a history of trash talk. The school wants an event that improves their relationship.
  1. How might another winner-takes-all contest affect the situation?
  2. Propose one design change that includes at least two favorable contact conditions.

Try it: which event would you design?

Another contest can strengthen the sense of "us versus them," especially if winning becomes a measure of group worth.

This includes equal status, a common goal, cooperation, and authority support. Kapwa also offers a lens for recognizing shared humanity across group labels.

Simply sharing a space does not guarantee better relations. Without shared work or equal footing, old patterns may persist.

See feedback

A possible concern: another contest can strengthen "us versus them".

A possible redesign: create mixed-section teams to produce one school service event. Give students equal roles, make the output depend on joint contributions, and ask teachers to support fair participation.

Concepts used: equal status, a common goal, cooperation, and authority support.

Practice

Your synthesis

Write one paragraph. Why do healthy relationships matter for well-being, learning, and working with others? Use at least three concepts from this lesson, add one example from adolescent life, and name one habit you will practice.

See a model response

One possible synthesis: Healthy relationships help us learn and feel supported because we can speak honestly and depend on one another. In a group project, individual accountability makes each person's contribution clear. Checking our attributions can stop us from labeling a struggling member lazy before asking what happened. Constructive conflict allows us to discuss a missed deadline without humiliation. I will practice asking about obstacles while agreeing on a clear next step.

What makes this work: it connects three concepts to a specific situation and ends with an observable habit. Your paragraph should use a different example and different concepts.
