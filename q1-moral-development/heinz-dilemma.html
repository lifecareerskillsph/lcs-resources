Life and Career Skills, Grade 11

The Heinz Dilemma

A classic instrument in moral development research. Read the story, choose the reasoning that comes closest to your own, and see what it reveals about how you think through right and wrong.

The Situation

In Europe, a woman was near death from a rare form of cancer. There was one drug doctors believed might save her: a form of radium a druggist in the same town had recently discovered. The druggist was charging $2,000 for a small dose, ten times what the drug cost him to make.

Heinz, the woman's husband, went to everyone he knew to borrow money, but could only raise $1,000, half of what he needed. He explained to the druggist that his wife was dying and asked him to sell it cheaper, or let him pay the rest later. The druggist refused: "No, I discovered the drug and I'm going to make money from it."

Desperate, Heinz broke into the man's store that night and stole the drug.

Should Heinz have done this? Read the twelve statements below. Choose the ONE that most closely matches how you would reason about it, not necessarily the one you agree with completely, but the one closest to your own thinking.

Facilitator note: in this mode, walk the class through the story aloud before revealing the statement list. Once statements are visible, give 3–4 minutes of silent reading before students select. Hold the group discussion prompts until after every student has submitted.

Choose Your Reasoning

Select one statement. There is no wrong answer; this measures how you reason, not whether you're "right."

Select a statement to continue.

Think Further

  1. Would your answer change if Heinz and his wife were strangers rather than married? Why or why not?
  2. Is there a difference between what is legal and what is moral in this situation? Where do you draw that line?
  3. Look at the level above yours in the reference table below. What would have to change in how you think for your reasoning to shift there?
Did you know? Kohlberg's postconventional stages (5 and 6) are the least commonly reached in longitudinal studies of adults, and reaching them isn't automatic with age, and most adult reasoning clusters in the conventional level. Landing in Level 1 or 2 here reflects how you reasoned about this one dilemma, not a fixed trait.
