The Porcupine and the Moles

A story about a shared space and a conflict of needs. How you resolve it says something about how you think conflicts should be settled: through rules, through relationships, or through rethinking the problem itself.

The Situation

It was growing cold, and a porcupine was looking for a home. He found a most desirable cave, but saw it was occupied by a family of moles.

"Would you mind if I shared your home for the winter?" the porcupine asked.

The generous moles agreed, and the porcupine moved in. But the cave was small, and every time the moles moved around, they were scratched by the porcupine's sharp quills. The moles endured the discomfort as long as they could. Then, at last, they gathered the courage to approach their visitor.

"Please leave," they said, "and let us have our cave to ourselves again."

"Oh no!" said the porcupine. "This place suits me very well. If you're not happy, then you should leave."

Imagine you are the judge called in to settle this dispute. Answer the three questions below in your own words before moving on.

Facilitator note: narrate the story aloud (or act it out with two volunteers) before students see the questions. Collect Question 2 answers verbally first, using a show of hands or quick poll, before revealing the orientation categories keeps the "creative third answer" possibility from being suggested too early.

As the Judge

Name the problem before you try to solve it.

What do you rule? Be specific.

Think about the exact conditions that would break your solution.

Think Further

  1. Would your resolution change if the porcupine had been living there first, and the moles moved in later?
  2. Look at the other two orientations in the reference table below. Write one sentence for how each of them would have resolved this, not just your own.
  3. Is there a real conflict in your life (family, friend group, org) where the "change the situation" approach might work better than picking a side?
Did you know? Carol Gilligan originally argued that Kohlberg's scoring system had no place for care-based or relationship-preserving reasoning, and that this made his model score some reasoning as "less developed" when it was really just a different orientation. Later meta-analytic research (Jaffee & Hyde, 2000) found justice and care orientations are used by all groups and depend more on the type of dilemma than on the person's gender: the split isn't fixed, it's situational.
