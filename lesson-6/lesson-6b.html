Learn

What mental health is

The World Health Organization describes mental health as a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well, work well, and contribute to their community (WHO, 2022). It does not say "never feeling bad." Mental health includes the capacity to feel, to cope, and to recover.

Two continua, not one

Keyes (2002) proposed that mental health and mental illness are related but separate dimensions. Someone with a diagnosed condition can be doing well in many areas of life. Someone with no diagnosis can be languishing: feeling empty, stuck, or disconnected. Someone who is flourishing reports positive feelings, positive functioning, and good relationships. Well-being deserves attention before a crisis, not only after one.

Learn

Stress and burnout in school and at work

Appraisal and coping

Stress depends on appraisal: whether you judge a demand as a threat or a challenge (primary appraisal) and whether you believe you have the resources to cope (secondary appraisal). The same exam can feel threatening to one student and manageable to another (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984).

CopingAimExamplesWorks best when
Problem-focusedChange the situation.Make a study schedule. Ask the teacher for help. Divide a project into tasks.There is something you can change.
Emotion-focusedManage the feeling.Breathing, talking to a friend, exercise, reappraising the situation.The situation cannot be changed right now, or the feeling blocks action.

Both are useful. Problems start when only one is used: all venting and no action, or all pushing through and no recovery.

Burnout

Maslach and Leiter (2016) describe three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism (a detached or negative attitude toward work or people), and reduced efficacy (feeling that nothing you do is effective). The WHO classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, not as a medical condition (WHO, 2019). Students show a similar pattern.

Burnout is not just "weak coping"

The Job Demands-Resources model explains burnout as an imbalance: high demands without enough resources (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). The question shifts from "What is wrong with me?" to "What is the balance of demands and resources, and what can change?" Relationships are among the most important resources. When unhealthy, they are also a source of demand.

Demands (drain)Resources (protect)
Heavy workload and short deadlines.Clear expectations and realistic planning.
Conflict with teachers, peers, or family.Supportive friends, mentors, and family.
Constant comparison with others.Sense of purpose, values, and progress.
Little control over schedule.Autonomy and choice where possible.
No time to rest.Sleep, movement, breaks, and hobbies.

Early warning signs (for noticing, not diagnosing)

  • Persistent tiredness that sleep does not fix.
  • Dreading tasks you used to handle, or growing cynicism about school.
  • Pulling away from friends and family.
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or concentration that last for weeks.
  • Feeling that nothing you do matters.

These can have many causes. If they persist, discuss them with a trusted adult or a counselor.

Workplace preview. Unclear roles, heavy workload, conflict, and low control also appear at work. Learning now to name demands and resources, ask for clarity, and set limits prepares you for internships and jobs. Not every stressor is solved by individual coping. Some require changes in the workplace or in policy (SDG 8).

Sources: Lazarus & Folkman (1984); Maslach & Leiter (2016); WHO (2019); Bakker & Demerouti (2007).

Pause B2: Camille's week

Facilitator noteUse this fictional case for breakout rooms. Do not ask students to disclose their own stress. Anonymous poll option: "Which demand is heaviest for you this month?"
Scenario. Camille is a Grade 11 student with a part-time online job, two group projects, and a family event this weekend. She used to enjoy her classes. Lately she feels numb, snaps at friends, and thinks, "Nothing I do is good enough."
  1. Which burnout dimensions can you see?
  2. List two demands and two resources in her situation.
  3. What problem-focused step and what emotion-focused step could she take this week? Who could help?
See a possible response

Dimensions: cynicism or detachment (numb, snapping) and reduced efficacy ("nothing is good enough"). Exhaustion is likely but not stated.

Demands: job, two group projects, family event. Resources: friends (if repaired), classmates, family, her own interest in classes.

Steps: problem-focused, list the deadlines and ask one teacher or groupmate for an adjusted timeline. Emotion-focused, a short walk or a talk with a trusted friend. Helpers: a trusted adult or school counselor. Consider whether the job hours can be reduced.

Learn

How relationships shape mental health

Relationships as protection

  • Buffering. In a classic review, Cohen and Wills (1985) found two ways social support helps: a general benefit of being connected, and a buffering effect that protects people when they face stress.
  • Survival and health. A meta-analysis of 148 studies found that people with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival over follow-up (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). This is an association.
  • Quality over quantity. Close friendship strength predicted rising self-worth and lower anxiety and depression from age 15 to 25, while broad popularity predicted more social anxiety (Narr et al., 2019). Peer support quality, not quantity, uniquely predicted depression (Letkiewicz et al., 2023).

Relationships as risk

Bullying, rejection, chronic conflict, controlling relationships, and online harassment are risk factors for distress. A relationship that leaves you constantly anxious, ashamed, or afraid is not protecting your mental health, even if it is long or familiar.

Filipino context: asking for help

A systematic review of Filipino help-seeking found that stigma, shame, concern about what others will think, and a preference for relying on oneself, family, or faith can act as barriers, while family and social support and trust in the helper can act as facilitators (Martinez et al., 2020). Hiya and pakikisama can make it hard to say "I am struggling." The same relational culture, pakikipagkapwa and kapamilya, can make support easy to reach when a person feels safe to ask. A healthy relationship is one where you can ask.

The Philippine Mental Health Act (RA 11036, 2018) affirms the rights of persons with mental health needs and promotes mental health services and education, including in schools and workplaces.

Where to get help. Start with someone you trust: a parent or guardian, teacher, adviser, or counselor. School support: UST CCC and school guidance and referral services. For urgent mental health support: NCMH Crisis Hotline 1553 (Smart/TNT: 0919-057-1553). If you or someone else may be in immediate danger, tell a trusted adult immediately and seek emergency assistance.
When a friend tells you they are struggling. Listen without rushing to fix. Thank them for trusting you. Do not promise secrecy if their safety is at risk. Encourage them to talk with a trusted adult or counselor and offer to go with them. Tell a trusted adult yourself if you are worried about their safety. You are a friend, not their therapist.

Sources: Cohen & Wills (1985); Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010); Narr et al. (2019); Letkiewicz et al. (2023); Martinez et al. (2020); RA 11036.

Learn

Self-care: what helps and what the evidence says

Self-care is a set of practices that protect your health. It is not selfishness, and it is not a substitute for professional help or for fixing an unreasonable workload. It is what you do on ordinary days to keep your resources up.

AreaExamplesNotes on evidence
Sleep and routineConsistent bedtime, screens down before sleep.Better sleep is associated with better mental health in adolescents (Lesson 2).
MovementWalking, sports, dance, arnis.Physical activity interventions are associated with lower depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Lesson 2).
Social connectionRegular time with people who leave you feeling accepted.Support quality matters more than numbers.
Thinking and feeling skillsNaming emotions, reappraising a situation, problem-solving.Reappraisal is a well-studied emotion regulation strategy (Gross, 1998).
Workload planningBreaking tasks down, protecting rest, saying no to extra tasks.Addresses demands directly.
Mindfulness and relaxationPaced breathing, body scan, prayer or reflection if meaningful to you.Mixed evidence. See the note below.
Seeking helpTelling a trusted adult, using school guidance.A skill, not a failure.
An honest note on mindfulness. Some studies show small benefits. But in the large MYRIAD trial of school-based mindfulness training in UK secondary schools, which included more than 8,000 students, students who received the program did not show better mental health outcomes at one year than students receiving usual school provision (Kuyken et al., 2022). This does not mean mindfulness is useless for everyone. It means it is one option among many, that fit and choice matter, and that a program delivered to everyone is not guaranteed to help everyone.

Limits of self-care. If distress is persistent, overwhelming, or unsafe, or if the cause is a situation you cannot change alone (bullying, abuse, severe overload), the next step is not more self-care. It is help from a trusted adult or a professional.

Sources: Gross (1998); Bakker & Demerouti (2007); Kuyken et al. (2022).

Signs: less sleep, skipped meals, and "done with everything" together are a pattern worth taking seriously, especially when he asks you to keep it secret.

Privacy and safety: privacy matters for ordinary things. When safety may be at risk, tell a trusted adult. You are a friend, not his therapist.

Possible words: "I am worried about you. I care about you too much to keep this just between us. Can we talk to the counselor together?" Trusted adults: a parent, adviser, or school counselor. If there is immediate danger, seek emergency help (NCMH 1553).

Recap

Part B: what to remember

  • Mental health is more than the absence of illness. Pay attention before a crisis.
  • Think in demands and resources. Burnout is exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy.
  • Quality of relationships matters more than quantity. Harmful relationships are a risk.
  • Self-care works best as a specific plan, and it has limits. Persistent or unsafe distress calls for a trusted adult or professional. NCMH 1553.
