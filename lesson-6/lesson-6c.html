Conflict Management

Part C of Establishing Healthy Relationships, with the lesson-wide synthesis. This is not graded. In Facilitated mode, notes appear before group activities; in Self-Paced mode, work through independently.

Learn

Where conflict shows up, and what makes it worse

In Lesson 5 you learned that conflict is normal and can be constructive or destructive. Part C teaches the skills. The curriculum names three: active listening, communication, and perspective-taking. This guide adds two that make them work: regulating your own emotion first, and problem-solving around interests.

Learning outcome: evaluate appropriate conflict management skills to improve personal and social relationships.

Four patterns that predict trouble

Gottman's research on couples identified four patterns that predict relationship breakdown (Gottman, 1994). The research is on couples, so apply it to other relationships as a caution, not a proven law. The suggested antidotes are useful anywhere.

PatternSounds likeAntidote
Criticism"You are so irresponsible."Gentle start-up: describe the situation and your feeling, and make a request.
ContemptEye-rolling, "Whatever, genius."Build a habit of appreciation and respect.
Defensiveness"It's not my fault, you did it too."Take responsibility for your part, even a small one.
StonewallingSilence, leaving the chat, walking out.Take a break and say when you will come back.

Sources: Gottman (1994).

Learn

The core skills

Skill 1: Regulate first

People rarely listen or reason well when flooded with anger or fear. Emotion regulation strategies work best when used early (Gross, 1998). Notice the body cues, breathe slowly, name the emotion, and if needed take a break. A break is not avoidance if you say when you will return: "I need ten minutes. Let's talk at 4."

Skill 2: Active listening

Rogers and Farson (1957) described active listening as listening for the full meaning, both content and feeling, and showing that you have understood.

MoveWhat it isExample
Pay attentionPut the phone down. Face the person. Do not plan your rebuttal.Eye contact and quiet.
ParaphraseSay back what you heard in your own words."So you felt left out when the plan changed without you."
Reflect feelingName the likely feeling and check it."It sounds like you were frustrated. Is that right?"
Ask open questionsInvite more, without judging."What would have helped?"
SummarizeCheck you have the whole picture before responding."Let me make sure I have this right..."

Active listening does not mean agreeing. It means the other person feels understood, which often lowers the temperature enough for problem-solving to begin. Common blocks: interrupting, advising too soon, minimizing ("It's not a big deal"), and changing the subject.

Skill 3: Say what you need clearly (I-messages)

Gordon (1970) popularized the I-message, which describes your experience rather than attacking the other person. Structure: When [specific behavior], I feel [feeling], because [impact]. Could we [request]?

You-message (attack)I-message (assertive)
"You never reply. You obviously don't care about this project.""When I don't hear back by evening, I feel worried because the deadline is Friday. Could we agree to reply within a day?"
"You're so loud and selfish.""When music is on while I study, I can't concentrate. Could we agree on quiet hours?"

Skill 4: Perspective-taking

Perspective-taking is trying to understand the other person's situation, goals, and reasoning, without having to agree. In negotiation experiments, parties who took the other side's perspective were better at finding agreements that served both sides (Galinsky et al., 2008). Prompts: "What might be going on for them?", "What would I want in their position?" This links to Lesson 5: checking for situational explanations reduces the fundamental attribution error.

Skill 5: Solve the problem, not the person

Fisher and Ury (1981) suggest four principles: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests and not positions, generate options for mutual gain, and use objective criteria. A position is what you say you want. An interest is why you want it. Two students who both say "I need the room" may find one needs it in the morning and the other in the afternoon.

Skill 6: Agree, repair, and follow up

  • Summarize the agreement in specific terms: who does what, by when.
  • Repair when harm was done: acknowledge what happened, take responsibility for your part, express regret, and say what you will do differently. Paghingi ng tawad and pakikipag-ayos are valued ways of restoring relationships.
  • Follow up. Set a time to check whether the agreement is working.
  • Bring in a third party when talking directly is not working: a peer mediator, adviser, counselor, or the barangay (Katarungang Pambarangay). A meta-analysis of school peer mediation found generally positive outcomes, including high rates of agreements and participant satisfaction (Burrell et al., 2003).

Sources: Gross (1998); Rogers & Farson (1957); Gordon (1970); Galinsky et al. (2008); Fisher & Ury (1981); Burrell et al. (2003).

Evaluating which skill fits

The outcome asks you to evaluate: ask whether a skill is appropriate, not only whether it is available.

CriterionQuestion to ask
EffectivenessDid it address the issue, not just calm the moment?
RelationshipIs the relationship intact or stronger afterward?
FairnessWere both sides heard, and is the outcome acceptable to both?
SafetyDid anyone feel unsafe, humiliated, or coerced?
SustainabilityWill the agreement hold, and can we repeat the process next time?
SituationLikely good fitWatch out for
Important group decision, time availableCollaborating, interest-based problem solving.Takes time.
Small matter, little at stakeAvoiding or accommodating for now.Resentment if it recurs.
Deadline tomorrow, goals are moderateCompromising.Neither side fully satisfied.
Emotions running highTake a break, then listen.Break becoming permanent silence.
Safety or urgent harmProtective action, adult involvement.Using force or shaming.
Repeated harm with power imbalanceNot conflict management. Report to an adult.Telling the target to "work it out" (see RA 10627).
When conflict management is the wrong tool. These skills assume two people with roughly equal power who are both safe. They are not appropriate when one person is threatening, controlling, stalking, abusing, or repeatedly humiliating another. Safety comes first: leave, tell a trusted adult, use the school's reporting system, and seek professional help. A person experiencing abuse is never responsible for "managing" it.

Practice

Pause C2: a joke in the group chat

Scenario. A classmate posts a joke about your presentation in a group chat. Others react with laughing emojis. You feel embarrassed and angry.
  1. Which Gottman pattern are you tempted to use? Which skill would you use first?
  2. Write a short, private message to the classmate using an I-message.
  3. Is this conflict or bullying? What would change your answer?

Try it: what would you do first?

This is contempt or defensiveness. It escalates in front of an audience and makes repair harder.

This regulates first, takes the issue out of the audience, and makes a specific request.

This is stonewalling. It protects you now but leaves the problem unresolved, and you lose the chance to repair.

See a possible response

Pattern and first skill: contempt or defensiveness are the temptations. Regulate first: breathe and take a break.

Possible message: "When the joke about my presentation was posted in the chat, I felt embarrassed and angry. Could you please delete it, and let me know if you have feedback about my slides?"

Conflict or bullying: one joke may be a conflict. Repeated targeting, a power imbalance, threats, or inability to make it stop point to bullying and call for an adult.

Practice

Pause C3: two leaders, one project

Scenario. Two members of your org want to lead the same project. Last week one told the other, "You always take over." They have not talked since. Both come to you for advice.
  1. Which Gottman patterns have already appeared?
  2. Describe how you would help them use at least three skills from this part.
  3. Use the five evaluation criteria: how would you know the outcome was good?
See a possible response

Patterns: "You always take over" is criticism. The silence since then is stonewalling.

Three skills: let each take a break and agree on a time to talk (regulate first), have each paraphrase the other (active listening), then ask what each needs from the project (interests, not positions) and look for options such as co-leads or dividing phases.

Evaluation: effectiveness (project assigned), relationship (they can still work together), fairness (both heard), safety (no humiliation), sustainability (a check-in date is set).

Recap

Lesson 6: what to remember

  • Values say what matters; strengths say what you can use. Relationships supply feedback, models, and belonging.
  • Communication, respect, and collaboration reduce process loss. Psychological safety lets groups learn.
  • Think in demands and resources. Quality of support matters more than quantity.
  • Self-care has limits. Persistent or unsafe distress calls for a trusted adult or professional. NCMH 1553.
  • Conflict skills: regulate, listen, I-message, take perspective, solve for interests, agree and repair.
  • Evaluate skills by effectiveness, relationship, fairness, safety, and sustainability. Conflict management is not for bullying, abuse, or threats.
