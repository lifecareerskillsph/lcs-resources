Learn

Psychosocial development: working definition

Psychosocial development is how identity, self-concept, relationships, values, and social functioning develop over time through interaction with other people and the social environment. It centers on three questions: who am I becoming (identity), what matters enough to guide my choices (values), and what kind of future fits who I am and what I value (career direction).

See the evidence behind this

Identity confusion is associated with higher depressive and anxiety symptoms during adolescence and emerging adulthood (Potterton et al., 2022). Feeling connected to school is associated with better mental health and lower risk across several areas (Rose et al., 2024). Social media can support peer connection and identity exploration, but can also increase exposure to social comparison, cyberbullying, and problematic use (Senekal et al., 2023).

Key idea: identity development is personal, but it is not purely individual. Relationships, school environments, culture, and online spaces can all shape how young people see themselves.

Learn

Filipino context: assets and tensions

Filipino relationships, family structures, and social expectations can shape identity in both supportive and challenging ways.

See the assets and tensions

Assets: pakikipagkapwa, identity develops in relationship with others; positive Filipino role models and mentors; family stories, shared values, and cultural transmission; support networks that help young people adapt when family members work away from home.

Tensions: generation gaps around identity, education, or career choices; social comparison linked to class, achievement, or perceived status; family separation due to overseas work, which may involve both self-reliance and emotional strain depending on family relationships and available support (Alcaide et al., 2024); online comparison, cyberbullying, and pressure to present an idealized self.

Sources: Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino (2000); Alcaide et al. (2024); Senekal et al. (2023).

Learn

Identity, belonging, and support

Identity does not create risk. The environment around a person can. Sexual and gender minority youth can experience higher mental health risk when they face stigma, rejection, bullying, victimization, or negative family treatment (de Lange et al., 2022). Supportive families, accepting peers, positive school relationships, and safe environments can help protect mental health and strengthen belonging (González et al., 2024).

Where support fits: if identity-related stress, rejection, bullying, or emotional distress begins to affect daily functioning, school, relationships, or safety, reaching out to a trusted adult or the school guidance and referral system is an appropriate next step.

Sources: de Lange et al. (2022); González et al. (2024); Meyer (2003).

Practice — real Philippine case

Case analysis: Bienvenido Lumbera

Bienvenido Lumbera was born in Lipa, Batangas in 1932 and lost both parents at an early age. He was raised by relatives and godparents, whose support became an important part of his development. Although he wanted to study at UP, practical considerations led him to enroll at UST, where he finished AB and MA Literature. He later earned a PhD in Comparative Literature at Indiana University and became a major Filipino writer, critic, teacher, and eventually National Artist for Literature. His life shows how identity and aspiration develop through both personal effort and social support.

  1. Identify one protective factor in Lumbera's development.
  2. Identify one risk factor or challenge.
  3. Classify each using the five-level framework.
  4. Explain how these factors may have influenced his identity, aspirations, and life choices.
  5. Which developmental task or tasks can you connect to his experience?
Psychosocial point: Lumbera's case shows that identity is not formed in isolation. Personal interests may begin within the self, but they are shaped by family circumstances, access to education, cultural influences, and social opportunity.

Sources: NCCA (n.d.); UP Alumni Association (2023).

Learn

Emotional development: working definition

This is not about never feeling upset. It is about regulation. Emotional development is the ability to experience, interpret, express, and regulate the full range of emotions: noticing what you feel, naming what it means, and regulating what you do about it.

See the evidence behind this

A systematic review of school-based adolescent interventions found improvements in emotion-regulation skills and mental health outcomes, with generally small-to-moderate effects and stronger effects in some higher-risk groups (Pedrini et al., 2022). Young people who believe emotions are more manageable tend to report fewer anxiety and depression symptoms, partly linked to greater use of adaptive strategies such as cognitive reappraisal, changing how you interpret a situation to change its emotional impact (Somerville et al., 2024).

Important — read before continuing

When to seek additional support

Some emotions are difficult but manageable. Additional support is important when distress becomes persistent, overwhelming, or unsafe. Consider reaching out when you notice: sadness, anxiety, irritability, or mood changes that persist for several weeks; difficulty managing emotions that begins to affect school, relationships, sleep, or daily functioning; worry or distress that feels difficult to control; withdrawal from usual activities or relationships; a noticeable decline in functioning; or thoughts of self-harm, suicide, or feeling that life is not worth living.

Where to get help
Start with someone you trust: parent or guardian, teacher, adviser, counselor, or another trusted adult.
School support: UST CCC / school guidance and referral services.
For urgent mental health support: NCMH Crisis Hotline: 1553 · Smart/TNT: 0919-057-1553.

If you or someone else may be in immediate danger, tell a trusted adult immediately and seek emergency assistance.

Mini synthesis: from body to identity

Paolo slept only 4 to 5 hours for three nights because of school deadlines. By the third day, he was irritable with friends, had difficulty concentrating, and started thinking, "Maybe I'm just not good at handling Senior High."
  1. Identify the pattern: what physiological or health-related pattern is present?
  2. Trace the effect: what emotional effect followed?
  3. Connect the domains: how did it begin to affect relationships or self-concept?
  4. Identify a protective response: what realistic factor or adjustment could help interrupt the pattern?

Practice — all three domains together

Integrated practice test

Sam is preparing for exams. For several nights, Sam sleeps only four hours, compares scores with classmates online, and begins feeling ashamed about asking for help. A class adviser notices that Sam has become withdrawn and invites Sam to check in privately.
  1. Physiological: identify one physiological risk factor in the scenario.
  2. Psychosocial: identify one psychosocial risk factor.
  3. Emotional: identify one emotional risk factor or difficulty.
  4. Protective factor: identify one protective factor that could help Sam, and classify its level.

Synthesis: explain how the physiological, psychosocial, and emotional factors may interact to affect Sam's well-being and school functioning.

See the answer key

Physiological: short sleep, exam fatigue, and likely late-night screen use.

Psychosocial: score comparison, concern about peer judgment, and withdrawal from others.

Emotional: shame, irritability, emotional overload, and difficulty seeking help.

Protective: the adviser's private check-in provides supportive adult contact and a possible referral pathway (Community/School level).

How the factors interact: short sleep leads to poorer regulation and concentration, which leads to stronger negative self-evaluation, which leads to withdrawal and reduced help-seeking.

Key idea: risk and protective factors can interact across physiological, psychosocial, and emotional development.

Recap

Lesson 2: what to remember

  • Adolescence involves tasks related to relationships, independence, values, responsibility, and future roles.
  • Protective and risk factors can operate at the Individual, Family, Community, Cultural, and Societal levels.
  • Physiological, psychosocial, and emotional development influence one another.
  • Patterns matter more than single moments.
  • Support can change the pathway. Protective factors do not remove every difficulty, but they can reduce risk and strengthen functioning.
