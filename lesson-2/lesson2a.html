What is a developmental task?

Havighurst proposed that developmental tasks arise from three main sources: physical maturation, personal values and aspirations, and societal expectations. Successfully working through a developmental task can support later development, while difficulty with the task may create challenges that carry forward (Havighurst, 1972).

See the three sources explained

Physical maturation: changes in the body and growing capacities create new developmental demands.

Personal values and aspirations: young people increasingly form their own values, goals, interests, and future aspirations.

Societal expectations: family, school, culture, peers, and wider society communicate expectations about roles and responsibilities.

Developmental tasks in today's context

Havighurst's framework is useful, but the path to adulthood has changed. Young people may feel pressure to "grow up" while education, work, housing, and other structural conditions can delay traditional adult milestones (Ratner, 2025). Family involvement can be protective, but excessive parental control is associated with poorer psychological, relational, academic, and career outcomes among emerging adults (Cui, Hong, & Jiao, 2022).

Key idea: becoming more independent does not require becoming disconnected from others. Growing autonomy can happen alongside connection, guidance, and support from family, peers, mentors, and other significant relationships.

The five-level lens, applied to physiological development

Protective and risk factors can operate at different levels of a young person's environment: Individual, Family, Community, Cultural, and Societal. A protective factor builds resilience and reduces risk; a risk factor increases the likelihood of harm to well-being.

LevelProtective factorsRisk factors
IndividualHealth literacy, consistent sleep routine, self-regulation skills, stress-management skills, regular physical activitySleep deprivation, sedentary behavior, excessive recreational screen time, substance use, poor nutrition
FamilyRegular shared meals, healthy routines modeled by caregivers, support for sleep and physical activity, access to nutritious foodIrregular meals, high family stress, limited health resources, unhealthy routines modeled at home
CommunitySafe spaces for physical activity, school health programs, supportive peers, accessible health servicesUnsafe environments, food insecurity, limited recreational spaces, peer norms that discourage healthy habits
CulturalFood traditions that support balanced eating, cultural practices that encourage movement, family and community care valuesStigma around help-seeking, restrictive gender expectations around activity, social norms that normalize unhealthy habits
SocietalAccess to public health services, safe transport and walkable environments, school policies that support rest and activity, affordable nutritious foodSocial and economic inequality, limited access to health services, high cost of nutritious food, digital environments designed to prolong screen use
Remember: a factor may operate at more than one level. Poor sleep, for example, may involve an Individual habit, a Family routine, and a Societal school or work schedule at the same time.
See the evidence behind this table

Better sleep is associated over time with better mental health and well-being in adolescents; sleep problems may also contribute to later behavioral difficulties, partly through emotion dysregulation (Bacaro et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Physical activity interventions are associated with lower depressive symptoms among children and adolescents (Recchia et al., 2023). Higher junk-food consumption is associated with greater psychological distress (Malmir et al., 2023). Higher recreational screen use is associated with shorter sleep duration and poorer sleep quality (Echevarria et al., 2023).

Filipino context: cultural assets and tensions

Assets: family and community support can strengthen health routines and help-seeking; familiar food practices can be a starting point for discussing balanced nutrition; shared activities such as basketball, arnis, dance, and community recreation can support movement and social connection.

Tensions: academic and family expectations may reduce time available for sleep, movement, or recovery; gender-role expectations may shape which activities feel acceptable or accessible; hiya, or concern about judgment, may sometimes make it harder to seek help for stress or health concerns (Enriquez, 1992; Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino, 2000).

Checkpoint: protective or risk?

Scenario: Mika regularly gets 8 to 9 hours of sleep despite a heavy academic workload. She notices that she is less irritable and more focused in class.
  1. Identify the factor: is Mika's sleep pattern functioning as a protective factor or a risk factor?
  2. Classify the level: at which level does this factor operate most directly?
  3. Explain the effect: how might consistent sleep support Mika's well-being and school functioning?
Compare your answer

Mika's sleep pattern is a protective factor operating at the Individual level. Consistent sleep supports emotion regulation, attention, and stress tolerance, which in turn supports school performance and stamina for future study or work.

Practice

Practice test: Arvin's late nights

Scenario: Arvin sleeps at 1:30 AM because he reviews, chats, and scrolls after class. His grades are still acceptable, but he has become more irritable, skips breakfast, and no longer plays basketball regularly.
  1. Identify one individual-level risk factor.
  2. Identify one family- or community-level protective factor that could help.
  3. Explain how this pattern could affect Arvin's well-being and readiness for future study or work.
See the answer key

Individual-level risk factor: insufficient sleep, prolonged late-night screen use, skipping breakfast, or reduced physical activity.

Protective factor: a consistent household sleep routine or agreement on late-night screen use (Family); better workload planning or access to school wellness support (Community/School).

Effect: insufficient sleep and disrupted health routines may affect attention, energy, mood regulation, consistency, and sustained performance, skills that matter for both current school functioning and future college or work demands.

Key idea: good academic performance does not automatically mean that a health pattern is protective.

Recap

What to remember from Week 4

  • Adolescence involves tasks related to relationships, independence, values, responsibility, and future roles.
  • Protective and risk factors can operate at the Individual, Family, Community, Cultural, and Societal levels.
  • Patterns matter more than single moments. Sleep, stress, and coping habits become protective or risk factors depending on context, not in isolation.
