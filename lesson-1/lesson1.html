Four lenses on the self

LC1: Examine your sense of self through developmental stages, tasks, and protective/risk factors. Developmental theories are maps for noticing patterns, not labels for people. A map can help you see patterns, but it cannot explain your whole life. Today's lesson works through four lenses: physical development, cognitive development, psychosocial development, and moral development.

Guiding question: How can developmental theory help you understand yourself without reducing yourself to a theory?
See the three-step process we'll use for each lens

Notice the pattern: recognize developmental patterns in everyday situations.

Name the lens: use the selected theory without turning it into a label.

Connect to self: explain what fits, what does not, and what context changes.

Practice

Where are you on the timeline?

This lesson focuses on the transition you are moving through: adolescence (10–19), emerging adulthood (18–25, a possible extended exploration period), and early adulthood (20–30, movement toward adult roles and commitments). These ranges overlap on purpose; the transition is not a hard line.

Prompt: Where do you feel you are right now, adolescent, adult, or in-between? Write a few sentences on what makes you feel that way.

Learn

1.1 Physical Development: same student, different situation

Start with judgment before naming the brain model. Consider the same student across three situations.

Situation A: Alone at home, exam tomorrow, an online game invite arrives.

Situation B: Friends are already online and repeatedly tagging you.

Situation C: You are tired, irritated, and stressed from school.

  1. Which situation makes it hardest to stick to the original plan?
  2. What changed between situations?
See the dual-systems explanation

The dual-systems model explains why planning and impulse can feel out of sync. The socioemotional / reward system handles reward, emotional intensity, social sensitivity, and risk-taking, especially in emotionally charged settings. The cognitive-control system handles planning, inhibition, decision-making, and long-term control, and it matures more gradually into the 20s. In socially or emotionally charged situations, the reward system can outpace the still-maturing control system: a mismatch, not a character flaw.

Plain version: regulation can be difficult, but responsibility and practice still matter.

Important science note: use the dual-systems model as a practical explanation, not a final explanation for every behavior. Do not translate it into "teenagers have no control." Brain networks are complex; motivation, context, sleep, stress, and relationships also matter. Practice and environmental support can strengthen regulation. The point is not excuse-making. The point is better self-management.

Sources: Steinberg (2008); Casey et al. (2008); Shulman et al. (2016); Pfeifer & Allen (2021).

Learn

Concrete thinking about real, present things

Before the pendulum problem shows you formal operational reasoning, it helps to see what comes right before it. Concrete operational thinking (roughly ages 7 to 11) is the ability to reason logically about physical objects that are actually present in front of you, using ideas like reversibility and compensation. It is a real cognitive advance, but it still stays anchored to what can be seen or touched.

Glass A is tall and thin. Glass B is short and wide. The same amount of milk is poured from Glass A into Glass B, and it now looks like less milk because the level is lower.

Does Glass B now have less milk, more milk, or the same amount? Explain your reasoning.
One kilogram of stone sits next to one kilogram of cotton. The cotton takes up far more space and looks much bigger.

Which one is heavier, or are they the same? Explain your reasoning.
See the concrete operational answer

Both amounts stay the same. Glass B looks like it has less milk only because the container is shorter and wider, not because any milk was added or removed, this is the idea of identity. The extra width also makes up for the lost height, this is compensation. And if you poured the milk back into Glass A, it would look exactly as it did before, this is reversibility. The same logic applies to the stone and cotton: nothing was added or removed, so one kilogram is one kilogram, regardless of how much space it takes up. A younger child in the preoperational stage typically centers on just one feature, the height of the glass, or the size of the cotton, and gets both questions wrong for that reason (Piaget, 1952).

Now push one step further, into formal operational territory: the stone and cotton weigh the same but take up very different amounts of space. What general property explains that difference, one that would hold true for any two materials, not just these two? Would the milk conservation still work if Glass B had a strange, irregular shape instead of a simple wide cylinder? A concrete operational answer handles the exact case in front of it. A formal operational answer reasons about the underlying principle well enough to predict cases it has not actually seen.

Sources: Piaget (1952).

Learn

Putting things in order, and into groups

Hand a four-year-old five sticks of different lengths and ask her to line them up from shortest to tallest. She gets the two shortest right, then loses track and puts a middle-length stick at the very end. Ask a nine-year-old to do the same thing, and he sorts all five correctly, often placing them in order almost without pausing.

What is the nine-year-old doing that the four-year-old cannot yet manage?
See the concept: seriation

Seriation is the ability to arrange objects in a logical, quantitative order along a dimension, such as length, weight, or size. The key difficulty is that each item in the series has to be held in mind two ways at once: the middle stick is simultaneously longer than the one before it and shorter than the one after it. A preoperational child usually manages one comparison at a time and loses the other, which is exactly what happened to the four-year-old above. A concrete operational child can hold both comparisons at once and sorts the full set systematically.

Show a child cards of a poodle, a labrador, a cat, and a sparrow, all mixed together, and ask, "Are there more dogs here, or more animals?" Many children younger than seven confidently answer, "More dogs."
See the concept: classification

Classification is the ability to group objects into categories and subcategories based on shared features. The more demanding version of this is hierarchical classification, understanding that a specific class, dogs, can be a subset of a broader class, animals, at the same time. A child who answers "more dogs" is comparing the subset to part of itself instead of to the whole group it belongs to. Concrete operational children grasp that a poodle is a dog and an animal simultaneously, without treating those as competing facts.

What actually makes a kid start thinking differently as they grow? Piaget's answer is called equilibration. It just means your brain wants things to make sense, and when they stop making sense, your brain gets to work fixing that.

There are two ways your brain can handle something new:
  • Assimilation, same schema: you use what you already know to handle something new, because it's close enough. Old knowledge plus new situation equals no real change. Example: you already know how to drive a sedan. You rent an SUV. You just drive it the same way you always have.
  • Accommodation, change the schema: what you already know doesn't work anymore, so your thinking has to change. Old knowledge plus new situation equals updated knowledge. Example: you have only ever driven an automatic car. Now you have to drive a stick shift. Your old habits don't work. You have to learn something new, like using a clutch.
When two ideas crash into each other, like expecting the tall glass to have more milk, then watching it get poured back and match exactly, that uncomfortable "wait, that doesn't add up" feeling is called disequilibrium. Equilibration is your brain sorting that discomfort out, and that is exactly how kids build skills like seriation, classification, and conservation, and eventually the kind of thinking the pendulum problem is about to ask of you.

Sources: Ship (n.d.); PsychologyNotesHQ (n.d.); Medical News Today (n.d.); Piaget (1977).

Practice Test: seriation, classification, and equilibration

Match each short scenario to the concept it best illustrates.

1. Ana is asked to sort ribbons from shortest to longest. She places three correctly, then loses track and puts a medium ribbon at the very end.
2. Given cards of a rose, a tulip, and a sunflower, Ben immediately calls all three flowers, then further sorts them into flowers with thorns and flowers without.
3. Mika calls every small, furry animal "kitten," including a rabbit she has never seen before, because it fits her existing idea of what a kitten looks like.
4. After her mother corrects her, Mika adjusts her mental categories so that rabbits and kittens are no longer the same thing.
5. A child expects the tall glass to have more milk, watches it get poured back into the original glass, sees the levels match exactly, and this discomfort pushes her to rethink how she reasons about quantity.
See the answer key

1. A, Ana is attempting to arrange items along a dimension, length, which is seriation, and her partial success shows the skill is not yet fully developed.

2. B, Ben is grouping by shared features and building a hierarchy within the group, which is classification.

3. C, Mika is fitting a new experience into a category she already has, which is assimilation.

4. D, Mika is changing the category itself because new information didn't fit, which is accommodation.

5. E, the conflict between expectation and observation, and the eventual resolution, describes equilibration as a whole, with the mismatch itself being the moment of disequilibrium.

Sources: Ship (n.d.); PsychologyNotesHQ (n.d.); Medical News Today (n.d.); Piaget (1977).

1.2 Cognitive Development: the pendulum problem

Experience formal operational thinking before naming it. A student wants to know what makes a pendulum swing faster: string length, object weight, release height, or strength of push.

How would you test which factor actually matters?
Compare your approach

A sound approach: change one thing at a time, keep the other factors constant, and compare the results. If you did something like this, you formed possibilities, controlled variables, predicted outcomes, and tested a hypothesis. Piaget called this formal operational reasoning: thinking about what could be, not only what is.

For Grade 11: the key shift is reasoning about possibilities, alternatives, and consequences. Concrete thinking asks "What is happening now?" Formal operational thinking asks "What could happen if...?" It means planning for possible futures, testing "what if" scenarios, thinking about identity, values, beliefs, and justice, and seeing contradictions and alternatives.

Learn

Two ideas both called egocentrism

Jane is three years old. She is shown a mountain and asked, "What is on top of the mountain in front of you?" She answers, "A house." Then she is asked, "What is on top of the mountain in front of John?" John is sixteen and is sitting across the table, looking at the same mountain from the opposite side. Jane answers, "A house," again, the exact same answer, even though John is looking at something Jane cannot see from where she sits.

Why do you think Jane gave the same answer both times?

Jane's answer is not a guess, and it is not something you could talk her out of by explaining it more clearly. At her age, she genuinely cannot yet mentally represent a viewpoint that differs from her own. From where she sits, there is a house on the mountain, so that is the only answer available to her, regardless of who is asking or where they happen to be sitting.

Piaget tested exactly this pattern with what is now known as the three mountains task: a child sits at a table facing a small model of three different mountains, a doll is placed on the opposite side of the table, and the child is shown several photos and asked which one shows what the doll sees. Children younger than about seven typically pick the photo that matches their own view, not the doll's, the same thing Jane just did (Piaget & Inhelder, 1956). This is preoperational egocentrism, a limit on ability, not a personality trait.

Hold that idea. The next section introduces "imaginary audience" and "personal fable," which also get called egocentrism, in a Grade 11 classroom, not a preschool. Before you read further, consider: could a fifteen-year-old, who can clearly describe what a friend or teacher is thinking, still be egocentric in some other sense?

The answer is yes, but the mechanism is different. Adolescent egocentrism is not a missing ability. A teenager can represent other people's perspectives just fine. What resurfaces instead is a temporary over-focus on the self, powered by the very formal operational thinking you are developing right now: the new capacity to reflect on your own thoughts also makes it easy to overestimate how much other people are thinking about you. One is a ceiling that has not yet been reached. The other is a new ability being pointed, for a while, mostly at yourself.

Sources: Piaget & Inhelder (1956); Elkind (1967).

Practice

Read these statements and infer the pattern they share: "My parents mean well, but they could never understand this situation." "Everyone will notice I wore these shoes twice this week." "Nobody has ever felt exactly what I am feeling." "I mispronounced one word. Everyone is probably still thinking about it."

See the pattern named

New abstract thinking can make self-consciousness more intense. Imaginary audience is the felt sense that others are constantly watching, noticing, or judging you. Personal fable is the felt sense that your experience is uniquely intense and others cannot really understand it. Naming the pattern while it is happening is already a self-awareness skill.

Sources: Piaget (1972); Elkind (1967).

4. Theo pauses before reacting online and says, "I feel watched, but that may not be the full truth."
See the answer key

1. B: Sofia reasons about possibilities and consequences, which shows formal operational thought.

4. E: Theo names the pattern while it is happening, which turns theory into self-awareness.

1.3 Psychosocial Development: four students, four decisions

Sort these cases before seeing the identity statuses named. Ask two questions for each: has this person explored, and has this person committed?

Aira: Explored courses, talked to people in different fields, and now chooses architecture because it fits her goals.
Ben: Will take accountancy because his family expects it. He has not seriously explored other paths.
Cara: Researches medicine, psychology, design, and law. She keeps exploring but has not committed yet.
Dan: Has not explored possible courses and says he will decide eventually.
See Marcia's identity statuses and check your sorting

Identity achievement (Aira): explored, then committed. Foreclosure (Ben): committed without much exploration. Moratorium (Cara): exploring, not yet committed. Diffusion (Dan): neither exploring nor committed.

These are identity statuses, not permanent labels. A person can move between them and may show different statuses in different areas of life. (Marcia, 1966)

Learn

Identity, intimacy, and emerging adulthood

Two Eriksonian psychosocial tasks become especially relevant across this transition: Identity vs. Role Confusion (adolescence), developing a clearer sense of who you are, what you value, and where you are headed; and Intimacy vs. Isolation (early adulthood), building close, committed relationships while maintaining a stable sense of self. A developing sense of identity supports deeper relationships, while relationships also help shape identity.

Arnett's emerging adulthood (roughly 18–25) is marked by identity exploration, instability in roles and plans, self-focus, a feeling of being in-between, and a sense that many futures are still open. It is not universal; it is easier to experience when social, cultural, and economic conditions allow extended exploration.

Filipino context: in many Asian contexts, identity exploration is shaped by family expectations, interdependence, and responsibility. Some young people take on work and adult responsibilities earlier, leaving less time for extended exploration. Exploration still happens, but it may unfold alongside kapamilya values, financial realities, and obligations to others.

Better question: which parts of the theory fit, for whom, and under what conditions?

Sources: Erikson (1968); Marcia (1966); Arnett (2000, 2015); Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino (2000).

See the answer key

2. B, Tin's frequent changes in plans and living situation describe instability.

3. C, Carlo is prioritizing his own process of figuring things out over an outside obligation, which is self-focus.

4. D, Bea's description of being neither fully a teenager nor fully an adult is the feeling of being in-between.

Sources: Arnett (2000, 2015).

1.4 Moral Development: the Heinz dilemma

A woman is dying. A drug might save her, but the pharmacist charges far more than Heinz can afford. Heinz asks to pay later, but the pharmacist refuses. Heinz then breaks into the store and steals the drug.
Choose one. Be ready to explain your reason.
Now the real question: what kind of reasoning are you using?

Reasoning sort. Read these six reasons and sort them by similar logic. Do not classify the YES or NO answer; classify the reason behind it.

  1. He might get arrested or punished.
  2. He should do it because he loves his wife.
  3. Stealing is against the law and could harm social order.
  4. A person's life matters more than property.
  5. Laws should protect people's rights and welfare.
  6. A moral principle may matter even when it conflicts with a rule.
See Kohlberg's three levels

Preconventional (reasons 1, 2): consequences, punishment, rewards, or personal gain.

Conventional (reasons 3, 5): approval, law, duty, and maintaining social order.

Postconventional (reasons 4, 6): rights, justice, conscience, and ethical principles.

Focus on the kind of reasoning, not just whether the action looks "good."

See Kohlberg's six stages

Stage 1, obedience and punishment: Will I be punished?

Stage 2, self-interest and exchange: What do I gain, or what is fair for me?

Stage 3, interpersonal approval: What would a "good" person do?

Stage 4, law and social order: What rules and duties keep society working?

Stage 5, social contract and rights: Do the rules protect people's rights and welfare?

Stage 6, universal ethical principles: What principle should guide me even if the law conflicts with it?

Practice

Justice and care + Practice Test 1.4

Gilligan argued that moral reasoning can emphasize more than rules and abstract principles. The justice ethic emphasizes fairness, rights, rules, and impartial principles. The care ethic emphasizes relationships, responsibility, context, and avoiding harm to others. Strong moral reasoning may draw on both.

Moral development is not only about what you choose. It is also about how you reason through conflict.

Sources: Kohlberg (1981); Gilligan (1982).

1. Mira asks who could be harmed, who needs protection, and how the relationship can be repaired responsibly.
2. A student refuses to spread a private screenshot because the person's dignity and rights matter even if others are laughing.
3. A class officer reports a violation because the class agreed on rules and everyone should follow them for order and fairness.
4. A student does not cheat because she is afraid of being caught and losing points.
See the answer key

1. E: Mira is focusing on harm, protection, responsibility, and repairing the relationship.

2. C: The student appeals to dignity and rights even when the peer group is doing otherwise.

3. B: The class officer emphasizes agreed rules, order, and fairness within the group.

4. A: The student avoids cheating mainly because of fear of being caught and punished.
