Key Developmental Stages: Understanding and Strengthening the Self

Self-paced work for Week 3. This is not graded. Go at your own pace, and use the See an example toggles whenever you want a worked example or answer key before trying something yourself.

Learn

Four lenses on the self

LC1: Examine your sense of self through developmental stages, tasks, and protective/risk factors. Developmental theories are maps for noticing patterns, not labels for people. A map can help you see patterns, but it cannot explain your whole life. Today's lesson works through four lenses: physical development, cognitive development, psychosocial development, and moral development.

Guiding question: How can developmental theory help you understand yourself without reducing yourself to a theory?
See the three-step process we'll use for each lens

Notice the pattern: recognize developmental patterns in everyday situations.

Name the lens: use the selected theory without turning it into a label.

Connect to self: explain what fits, what does not, and what context changes.

Practice

Where are you on the timeline?

This lesson focuses on the transition you are moving through: adolescence (10–19), emerging adulthood (18–25, a possible extended exploration period), and early adulthood (20–30, movement toward adult roles and commitments). These ranges overlap on purpose; the transition is not a hard line.

Prompt: Where do you feel you are right now — adolescent, adult, or in-between? Write a few sentences on what makes you feel that way.

Learn

1.1 Physical Development: same student, different situation

Start with judgment before naming the brain model. Consider the same student across three situations.

Situation A: Alone at home, exam tomorrow, an online game invite arrives.

Situation B: Friends are already online and repeatedly tagging you.

Situation C: You are tired, irritated, and stressed from school.

  1. Which situation makes it hardest to stick to the original plan?
  2. What changed between situations?
See the dual-systems explanation

The dual-systems model explains why planning and impulse can feel out of sync. The socioemotional / reward system handles reward, emotional intensity, social sensitivity, and risk-taking, especially in emotionally charged settings. The cognitive-control system handles planning, inhibition, decision-making, and long-term control, and it matures more gradually into the 20s. In socially or emotionally charged situations, the reward system can outpace the still-maturing control system: a mismatch, not a character flaw.

Plain version: regulation can be difficult, but responsibility and practice still matter.

Important science note: use the dual-systems model as a practical explanation, not a final explanation for every behavior. Do not translate it into "teenagers have no control." Brain networks are complex; motivation, context, sleep, stress, and relationships also matter. Practice and environmental support can strengthen regulation. The point is not excuse-making. The point is better self-management.

Sources: Steinberg (2008); Casey et al. (2008); Shulman et al. (2016); Pfeifer & Allen (2021).

Practice

Practice Test 1.1: Physical development

Late adolescence and early adulthood can be distinguished by broad markers: puberty completion, growth-rate stabilization, and motor-skill refinement. Identify the developmental idea shown in each situation.

1. Nico promises to sleep early before a quiz, but joins an online game late at night when friends invite him.
2. Aly notices that repeated volleyball drills improved her timing, balance, and accuracy.
3. Mika is no longer getting taller as quickly as before, and body changes feel more stable than in junior high school.
4. Ken puts his phone across the room before sleeping so he has time to think before reacting to notifications.
See the answer key

1. D — Nico shows reward/control mismatch: immediate social reward becomes stronger than his plan to rest.

2. C — Aly describes motor-skill refinement through repeated practice and coordination.

3. B — Mika shows growth-rate stabilization, where rapid physical growth begins to slow or settle.

4. E — Ken uses a protective habit that gives the control system time to act before impulse takes over.

Learn

1.2 Cognitive Development: the pendulum problem

Experience formal operational thinking before naming it. A student wants to know what makes a pendulum swing faster: string length, object weight, release height, or strength of push.

How would you test which factor actually matters?
Compare your approach

A sound approach: change one thing at a time, keep the other factors constant, and compare the results. If you did something like this, you formed possibilities, controlled variables, predicted outcomes, and tested a hypothesis. Piaget called this formal operational reasoning: thinking about what could be, not only what is.

For Grade 11: the key shift is reasoning about possibilities, alternatives, and consequences. Concrete thinking asks "What is happening now?" Formal operational thinking asks "What could happen if...?" — planning for possible futures, testing "what if" scenarios, thinking about identity, values, beliefs, and justice, and seeing contradictions and alternatives.

Practice

Scenario sort + Practice Test 1.2

Read these statements and infer the pattern they share: "My parents mean well, but they could never understand this situation." "Everyone will notice I wore these shoes twice this week." "Nobody has ever felt exactly what I am feeling." "I mispronounced one word. Everyone is probably still thinking about it."

See the pattern named

New abstract thinking can make self-consciousness more intense. Imaginary audience is the felt sense that others are constantly watching, noticing, or judging you. Personal fable is the felt sense that your experience is uniquely intense and others cannot really understand it. Naming the pattern while it is happening is already a self-awareness skill.

Sources: Piaget (1972); Elkind (1967).

Match each situation to the thinking pattern.

1. Sofia lists three possible senior high pathways and what could happen if she chooses each one.
2. Paolo stumbles during a class report and feels that everyone will remember it for weeks.
3. Lara says nobody can understand how intense this feels because nobody has gone through something like this.
4. Theo pauses before reacting online and says, "I feel watched, but that may not be the full truth."
See the answer key

1. B — Sofia reasons about possibilities and consequences, which shows formal operational thought.

2. C — Paolo assumes others are closely watching and judging him, which is the imaginary audience pattern.

3. D — Lara feels her experience is uniquely intense and beyond others' understanding, which reflects the personal fable.

4. E — Theo names the pattern while it is happening, which turns theory into self-awareness.

Learn

1.3 Psychosocial Development: four students, four decisions

Sort these cases before seeing the identity statuses named. Ask two questions for each: has this person explored, and has this person committed?

Aira: Explored courses, talked to people in different fields, and now chooses architecture because it fits her goals.
Ben: Will take accountancy because his family expects it. He has not seriously explored other paths.
Cara: Researches medicine, psychology, design, and law. She keeps exploring but has not committed yet.
Dan: Has not explored possible courses and says he will decide eventually.
See Marcia's identity statuses and check your sorting

Identity achievement (Aira): explored, then committed. Foreclosure (Ben): committed without much exploration. Moratorium (Cara): exploring, not yet committed. Diffusion (Dan): neither exploring nor committed.

These are identity statuses, not permanent labels. A person can move between them and may show different statuses in different areas of life. (Marcia, 1966)

Learn

Identity, intimacy, and emerging adulthood

Two Eriksonian psychosocial tasks become especially relevant across this transition: Identity vs. Role Confusion (adolescence), developing a clearer sense of who you are, what you value, and where you are headed; and Intimacy vs. Isolation (early adulthood), building close, committed relationships while maintaining a stable sense of self. A developing sense of identity supports deeper relationships, while relationships also help shape identity.

Arnett's emerging adulthood (roughly 18–25) is marked by identity exploration, instability in roles and plans, self-focus, a feeling of being in-between, and a sense that many futures are still open. It is not universal; it is easier to experience when social, cultural, and economic conditions allow extended exploration.

Filipino context: in many Asian contexts, identity exploration is shaped by family expectations, interdependence, and responsibility. Some young people take on work and adult responsibilities earlier, leaving less time for extended exploration. Exploration still happens, but it may unfold alongside kapamilya values, financial realities, and obligations to others.

Better question: which parts of the theory fit, for whom, and under what conditions?

Sources: Erikson (1968); Marcia (1966); Arnett (2000, 2015); Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino (2000).

Practice

Practice Test 1.3: Identity or transition pattern

1. Jia compared courses, talked to people in different fields, and now commits to a health-related strand because it fits her values.
2. Marco will take the same course his family chose for him, but has never explored whether it fits him.
3. Ana is trying clubs, asking career questions, and changing her mind often. She has not chosen yet.
4. Luis expects a period of trying work, study, and personal interests before settling into adult roles, if his family situation allows it.
See the answer key

1. A — Jia explored options and made a commitment, which fits identity achievement.

2. B — Marco shows commitment without much exploration, which fits foreclosure.

3. C — Ana is actively exploring but has not made a firm commitment, which fits moratorium.

4. E — Luis describes emerging adulthood as a possible exploration period, while noting that context matters.

Learn

1.4 Moral Development: the Heinz dilemma

A woman is dying. A drug might save her, but the pharmacist charges far more than Heinz can afford. Heinz asks to pay later, but the pharmacist refuses. Heinz then breaks into the store and steals the drug.
Choose one. Be ready to explain your reason.
Now the real question: what kind of reasoning are you using?

Reasoning sort. Read these six reasons and sort them by similar logic. Do not classify the YES or NO answer; classify the reason behind it.

  1. He might get arrested or punished.
  2. He should do it because he loves his wife.
  3. Stealing is against the law and could harm social order.
  4. A person's life matters more than property.
  5. Laws should protect people's rights and welfare.
  6. A moral principle may matter even when it conflicts with a rule.
See Kohlberg's three levels

Preconventional (reasons 1, 2): consequences, punishment, rewards, or personal gain.

Conventional (reasons 3, 5): approval, law, duty, and maintaining social order.

Postconventional (reasons 4, 6): rights, justice, conscience, and ethical principles.

Focus on the kind of reasoning, not just whether the action looks "good."

See Kohlberg's six stages

Stage 1, obedience and punishment: Will I be punished?

Stage 2, self-interest and exchange: What do I gain, or what is fair for me?

Stage 3, interpersonal approval: What would a "good" person do?

Stage 4, law and social order: What rules and duties keep society working?

Stage 5, social contract and rights: Do the rules protect people's rights and welfare?

Stage 6, universal ethical principles: What principle should guide me even if the law conflicts with it?

Practice

Justice and care + Practice Test 1.4

Gilligan argued that moral reasoning can emphasize more than rules and abstract principles. The justice ethic emphasizes fairness, rights, rules, and impartial principles. The care ethic emphasizes relationships, responsibility, context, and avoiding harm to others. Strong moral reasoning may draw on both.

Moral development is not only about what you choose. It is also about how you reason through conflict.

Sources: Kohlberg (1981); Gilligan (1982).

1. Mira asks who could be harmed, who needs protection, and how the relationship can be repaired responsibly.
2. A student refuses to spread a private screenshot because the person's dignity and rights matter even if others are laughing.
3. A class officer reports a violation because the class agreed on rules and everyone should follow them for order and fairness.
4. A student does not cheat because she is afraid of being caught and losing points.
See the answer key

1. E — Mira is focusing on harm, protection, responsibility, and repairing the relationship.

2. C — The student appeals to dignity and rights even when the peer group is doing otherwise.

3. B — The class officer emphasizes agreed rules, order, and fairness within the group.

4. A — The student avoids cheating mainly because of fear of being caught and punished.

Recap

Putting Lesson 1 together

Development is multi-layered: Physical (body + brain), Cognitive (thinking + self-consciousness), Psychosocial (identity + relationships), and Moral (values + responsibility). No single theory explains the whole person.
