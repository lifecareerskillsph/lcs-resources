This guide organizes the material covered on the 1st Quarterly Examination by lesson, states the underlying construct behind each pattern of question you can expect, and adds new items at matching difficulty for further practice. It is a diagnostic and review tool: none of the practice items below are actual test items.

Where an item straddles two constructs (a Havighurst scenario resolved through an Erikson term, a wellness scenario framed as a developmental-stage claim), the mapping follows what the correct answer actually turns on, not the surface content of the scenario. This matters for review sequencing more than for scoring: a student weak on Lesson 1 theory but strong on Lesson 2 application will miss different items than the raw topic label suggests.

Lesson 1: Key Developmental Stages

Pointers for Review

Erikson's psychosocial stages

  • Two stages carry this exam: Identity vs. Role Confusion (adolescence) and Intimacy vs. Isolation (young adulthood).
  • Sequence is directional: identity work precedes the capacity for genuine intimacy, not the reverse. "Establish intimacy, then identity" sequencing is always the wrong-order distractor.
  • Each stage resolves toward a named virtue on successful resolution: the autonomy-stage virtue is will, and the identity-stage virtue is fidelity. Expect a virtue name used as a distractor label attached to the wrong stage.

Marcia's identity statuses (extends Erikson)

  • Built on two dimensions: exploration (has the person actively examined options?) and commitment (has the person settled on a choice?).
StatusExplorationCommitment
AchievementYesYes
MoratoriumYesNot yet
ForeclosureNoYes (adopted from others)
DiffusionNoNo
  • Access and opportunity constrain which status is reachable: lack of access limits exploration, but it does not guarantee diffusion, and interest without access does not guarantee achievement either.

Arnett's emerging adulthood

  • A period (not a fixed age band tested here) marked by identity exploration across love, work, and worldview, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of open possibility.
  • Self-focus is not selfishness. Instability is not failure. The whole period functions as an extended psychosocial moratorium: normal, not a developmental problem to be explained away.

Piaget's formal operational stage

  • Defined by abstract, hypothetical, and systematic reasoning.
  • Two traps recur: (1) formal reasoning is not uniformly applied across every content domain, since familiarity and interest shift how abstractly a person reasons about a given topic; (2) struggling with one unfamiliar problem does not prove the stage has not been reached.
  • Equilibration: the process of restoring cognitive balance after new information disrupts an existing schema, producing a more complex, revised belief (not simple discomfort, and not a synonym for accommodation alone).

Kohlberg's stages of moral reasoning

LevelStageOrientation
Preconventional1Obedience and punishment avoidance
Preconventional2Self-interest, instrumental exchange
Conventional3Interpersonal approval ("good boy/girl")
Conventional4Law and order, social-order maintenance
Postconventional5–6Social contract; universal ethical principles
  • Preconventional reasoning is tied to consequences (reward/punishment), not intention. "Intention determines right/wrong" is a Stage 3+ idea being misattributed to preconventional reasoning when it appears in a True/False item.
  • Identical behavior can come from different stages; the exam repeatedly tests that same action does not equal same stage.

Gilligan's ethics of care

  • Individual survival (self-protective orientation) → self-sacrifice (goodness equated with sacrificing for others, still conventional) → integrated care / principle of nonviolence (balances care for self and others, postconventional).
  • The correct answer to an integration item pairing Kohlberg and Gilligan is almost always the option that credits both a justice/fairness concern and a care-for-self-and-others concern, not one to the exclusion of the other.

Biological maturation and executive function

  • Physical/pubertal maturity is not synchronized with prefrontal cortex maturation, which continues into the mid-twenties. Visible physical adulthood does not imply adult-level impulse control or planning.

Item design pattern

Most Lesson 1 items are integration items: two theorists are combined in one scenario, and the question asks which interpretation "BEST integrates" both. The wrong options typically apply only one framework, or reframe a normal developmental process (exploration, instability, self-focus) as failure or pathology.

Test-taking strategy: the absolute-qualifier heuristic

Most of Lesson 1's True/False items follow the same construction: state a true theoretical claim, then attach a word that overreaches it. A student can catch a meaningful share of these without having mastered the underlying theory, simply by scanning for the word doing the work: "always," "never," "all," "none," "must," "guarantees," "necessarily," "consistently in every situation." Qualified language ("often," "may," "can," "generally," "more likely") is the opposite signal and tends to mark a true statement.

Worked example

"Reaching the formal operational stage means that an adolescent will use abstract and logical thinking consistently in every situation." The theory is accurate; the absolute claim after it is not.

"If two people make the same moral decision, they must be operating at the same level of moral reasoning." Same mechanism: "must" is the tell.

A statement that restates the identical trap with "necessarily place them at the same moral stage" works the same way.

Because this is a test-taking skill rather than content knowledge, it is worth practicing explicitly and separately from the theory itself, especially when review time is short: it transfers across any exam written with this convention. It is not a substitute for understanding the theory, though; it only works on items built this way, and Lesson 2's True/False items are built differently (see below).

Practice Items: Lesson 1

P1. A 19-year-old has enrolled in and dropped two college programs in two years. She says she is "not ready to decide" but is actively attending career fairs and interviewing professionals in three different fields. Which identity status BEST describes her situation?

  • A. Foreclosure
  • B. Diffusion
  • C. Moratorium
  • D. Achievement

Rationale: Active exploration without a settled commitment is the defining pattern of moratorium, regardless of how long it takes.

P2. A father tells his son, "You will take over the family hardware business; there is nothing else to consider," and the son agrees without ever researching or trying an alternative. Which identity status is illustrated?

  • A. Achievement
  • B. Foreclosure
  • C. Moratorium
  • D. Diffusion

Rationale: Commitment without prior exploration, adopted wholesale from another person's expectations, is foreclosure.

P3. At 24, Miguel has changed jobs twice while clarifying what kind of work fits him, dates casually while figuring out what he wants in a partner, and is reconsidering beliefs he grew up with. Which interpretation BEST integrates Arnett and Erikson?

  • A. His instability signals a failure to achieve identity by adulthood
  • B. He is engaging in emerging-adulthood exploration that precedes consolidating identity toward intimacy
  • C. His self-focus is inherently selfish and is delaying healthy development
  • D. Questioning inherited beliefs indicates unresolved role confusion that cannot be outgrown

Rationale: This is textbook emerging adulthood: exploration across work, relationships, and worldview, functioning as a moratorium on the way toward identity and eventually intimacy, not evidence of failure.

P4. A 17-year-old debates the ethics of a school policy using abstract principles with ease, but cannot work through an unfamiliar geometry proof without concrete examples. Which conclusion is BEST supported?

  • A. She has not reached formal operational thought
  • B. Formal reasoning is domain-specific and can be expressed unevenly depending on familiarity
  • C. Her ability in ethics proves general cognitive superiority
  • D. She is regressing to concrete operational thought

Rationale: Uneven performance across domains reflects familiarity and interest, not the absence of formal operational capacity.

P5. A student refuses to help a classmate cheat, reasoning only that "cheating breaks a rule everyone benefits from having," without weighing the classmate's circumstances or considering any exception. Which Kohlberg stage is BEST represented?

  • A. Stage 6 (universal ethical principles)
  • B. Stage 4 (law and order)
  • C. Stage 3 (interpersonal accord)
  • D. Stage 1 (obedience and punishment)

Rationale: Appeal to a fixed rule that maintains social order, without weighing relationships or exceptions, is Stage 4 conventional reasoning.

P6. A team captain declines to help an injured rival athlete during a meet, explaining that helping "wouldn't benefit me in any way." Which orientation in Gilligan's framework BEST fits this reasoning?

  • A. Integrated care
  • B. Self-sacrifice
  • C. Individual survival
  • D. Postconventional care

Rationale: Reasoning centered entirely on personal benefit or cost, with no reference to the other's need or to a balance between self and others, is the self-protective individual survival orientation.

P7. A 19-year-old can explain the long-term financial consequences of debt clearly in a classroom discussion, yet overspends impulsively when he receives a bonus. Which explanation is MOST consistent with adolescent and young-adult brain development?

  • A. Knowledge should automatically produce self-control at this age
  • B. The prefrontal cortex, responsible for impulse regulation, continues maturing into the mid-twenties, so knowing and doing can diverge
  • C. This proves a permanent decision-making deficit
  • D. Financial literacy has no relationship to impulse control

Rationale: Cognitive knowledge and impulse regulation are governed by different, unevenly maturing systems; the gap between the two is expected, not pathological, at this age.

P8. After believing "leaders must always be confident," a student leads a group project poorly, feels unsettled by the mismatch, and later revises her belief to "leadership requires both confidence and the willingness to ask for help." Which process does this illustrate?

  • A. Assimilation only
  • B. Foreclosure
  • C. Equilibration
  • D. Identity diffusion

Rationale: Disequilibrium followed by a revised, more complex belief is equilibration: the restoration of cognitive balance after new experience disrupts an existing schema.

True/False: Lesson 1

P9. Erikson's stage sequence places the resolution of Identity vs. Role Confusion before Intimacy vs. Isolation.

True

Rationale: Identity work precedes the capacity for genuine intimacy in Erikson's sequence.

P10. A person can display Stage 3 moral reasoning in one context (family loyalty) and Stage 4 reasoning in another (workplace rules) within the same week.

True

Rationale: Moral reasoning, like formal operational reasoning, is not perfectly uniform across every context a person encounters.

P11. Reaching Arnett's emerging adulthood period guarantees that a person will resolve Erikson's identity crisis by age 25.

False

Rationale: Emerging adulthood describes a period of active exploration; it does not guarantee resolution within a fixed age window.

P12. Gilligan's "individual survival" orientation and Kohlberg's preconventional level are similar in that both center primarily on consequences to the self rather than on relationships or abstract principles.

True

Rationale: Both frameworks locate their earliest orientation in self-focused, consequence-driven reasoning before relational or principled reasoning develops.

Lesson 2: Developmental Tasks & Protective/Risk Factors

Pointers for Review

Havighurst's developmental tasks

  • Eight tasks, arising from three sources: physical maturation, personal values & aspirations, societal expectations. A single task is often shaped by more than one source at once, so trace the scenario back to its source rather than assuming a task has only one origin.
  • Two of the original eight (achieving a masculine/feminine social role, and preparing for marriage and family life) are treated in this curriculum as historical formulations of their era, not universal expectations. Expect items that ask you to reinterpret them through a contemporary, individual-differences lens rather than apply them literally.
  • Contemporary emotional independence means making one's own decisions while still being able to seek advice from trusted adults. Neither total self-reliance (refusing all input) nor total dependence (letting others decide) satisfies this task; both are common wrong-answer poles.

Five-level Protective/Risk Factor framework

  • Levels: Individual, Family, Community, Cultural, Societal, applied consistently across physiological, psychosocial, and emotional development.
  • A factor can operate at more than one level at once. A strong factor at one level does not cancel a barrier at another: a family that models healthy meals does not remove a societal-level food-affordability problem.
  • Core probabilistic principle, tested repeatedly: no single protective factor guarantees a positive outcome, and no single risk factor guarantees a negative one. Development is multi-level and cumulative, not deterministic from one variable.

Self-concept and emotional self-regulation

  • Conditions of worth (Rogers): a family-level risk pattern where approval is contingent on performance or mood-matching, producing hypervigilance to others' emotional states instead of self-directed development.
  • The three-step emotional intelligence process tested here: recognize the feeling → identify the thought behind it → choose a response. Skipping to rationalization, or substituting suppression for pausing, both fail this sequence.
  • A realistic reframe acknowledges the negative emotion while adding perspective: it neither denies the emotion ("I shouldn't feel this") nor externalizes all responsibility ("it's entirely someone else's fault").
  • Communication styles: passive (no self-expression, avoids conflict at personal cost), aggressive (self-expression at others' expense), assertive (clear self-expression with respect for both parties). The exam's default correct answer for handling conflict is assertive, "I"-statement communication.
  • Empathy vs. sympathy: empathy validates another's perspective even while holding a different view of the situation; sympathy is more distanced, feeling sorry for someone without necessarily engaging with their perspective.

Filipino cultural concepts, used analytically

  • Bayanihan, pakikipagkapwa, utang na loob, hiya, kamustahan: each is tied to a specific level (usually Cultural) and a specific mechanism (belonging, obligation, restraint, connection). Naming the concept without stating its mechanism will not carry a scenario item; the exam expects the mechanism.

Item design pattern

Many items present a plausible-sounding conclusion ("His conclusion is reasonable because...") and ask for an evaluation. The correct answer usually names what the conclusion leaves out (a missing level, an ignored red flag, an assumed guarantee) rather than simply agreeing or flatly rejecting it.

Why the Lesson 1 shortcut mostly does not work here

The absolute-qualifier heuristic taught under Lesson 1 (scan for "always / never / must / guarantees / necessarily") only catches a small minority of this lesson's True/False items. One clean exception uses the same mechanism as the Lesson 1 items, testing the identical probabilistic principle about a single protective factor guaranteeing an outcome. Most others show why the shortcut generally fails:

Worked example

"Friends who normalize staying up late, substance use, or excessive screen time can function as community-level risk factors even when those behaviors are common in the peer group." There is no absolute word to flag, and the statement is true. The "even when" clause tests whether the student understands that normalcy within a peer group does not cancel a factor's risk status; it is a scenario built to feel like it shouldn't count as risk, precisely because "everyone does it."

This is why Lesson 2 review time should go toward internalizing the five-level framework and practicing it on cases designed to feel counterintuitive, rather than toward wording-pattern drills: it is a slower thing to teach, which is also why the practice set below leans toward scenario-application items rather than pure recall.

Practice Items: Lesson 2

P1. A student says, "I only feel good about myself when I get perfect scores; when I don't, I feel worthless." Which family-level risk pattern is MOST likely underlying this belief?

  • A. Emotional dismissal
  • B. Conditions of worth
  • C. Excessive monitoring
  • D. Identity foreclosure

Rationale: Self-worth made contingent on performance is the defining pattern of conditions of worth.

P2. A barangay builds a covered court, funds a values-formation program for out-of-school youth, and partners with a state university for scholarship slots. Which levels of protective factors are MOST directly combined?

  • A. Individual and Family
  • B. Community and Societal
  • C. Cultural and Individual
  • D. Family and Cultural

Rationale: Local infrastructure and programming operate at the Community level; a partnership creating access to higher education operates at the Societal level.

P3. A father tells his daughter, "Ask your titos and titas for their advice, but the decision is yours to make." Which contemporary interpretation of a Havighurst task does this BEST reflect?

  • A. Achieving emotional independence by deciding for oneself while still consulting trusted adults
  • B. Achieving a gender-related social role
  • C. Preparing for marriage and family life
  • D. Emotional independence requiring total self-reliance

Rationale: This is the contemporary reading of emotional independence tested throughout the exam: autonomy in decision-making alongside, not instead of, trusted adult input.

P4. A community has strong bayanihan practices, but its only clinic closed two years ago, and the nearest hospital is three hours away. Which statement BEST evaluates the situation?

  • A. Bayanihan fully compensates for the lack of health services
  • B. The cultural protective factor does not remove the societal-level barrier to healthcare access
  • C. Community protective factors always outweigh societal risk factors
  • D. The lack of a clinic is irrelevant if community cohesion is high

Rationale: A strong factor at one level does not cancel a barrier operating at a different level: the core probabilistic principle of the framework.

P5. A teacher tells an anxious student, "You're not stressed, you're just being dramatic." Which response would instead BEST demonstrate the recognize-identify-respond emotional intelligence process?

  • A. "You shouldn't feel that way, so let's move on."
  • B. "Notice what your body is doing right now, name the thought behind it, then decide how you want to respond."
  • C. "Ignore the feeling and focus only on the facts."
  • D. "Feelings and thoughts are the same thing, so just think differently."

Rationale: This follows the correct sequence: physiological/emotional recognition, then the thought behind it, then a chosen response, without suppressing the feeling or skipping straight to rationalization.

P6. Which statement is the MOST realistic reframe of failing a driving test on the first attempt?

  • A. "This means I'll never be a good driver."
  • B. "I'm frustrated, but one attempt doesn't determine my ability to eventually pass."
  • C. "I shouldn't feel frustrated since it's just a test."
  • D. "The examiner was clearly unfair, so nothing on my end needs to change."

Rationale: A realistic reframe holds the negative emotion and adds perspective, without denying the feeling or externalizing all responsibility.

True/False: Lesson 2

P7. A single strong protective factor at the Individual level, such as high self-esteem, is generally sufficient to guarantee a positive developmental outcome regardless of risk exposure at other levels.

False

Rationale: No single factor at any one level guarantees an outcome; development is cumulative across levels.

P8. Utang na loob can function protectively when it motivates genuine reciprocity, but can function as a risk when it is used to obligate someone against their own values or boundaries.

True

Rationale: The same cultural concept can carry a protective or a risk mechanism depending on how it operates in the relationship.

P9. "I hear that you're upset, though I don't see the situation the same way" is an example of sympathy rather than empathy.

False

Rationale: This is empathy: validating another's perspective while holding a different view. Sympathy is feeling sorry for someone without necessarily engaging with their perspective.

P10. A protective factor operating at the Family level can never also operate at the Community level.

False

Rationale: A factor such as supportive relationships can operate at more than one level simultaneously.

Lesson 3: Promoting Physiological Development & Well-Being

Pointers for Review

Mindfulness

  • Defined as present-moment, non-judgmental awareness of thoughts, feelings, bodily sensations, and environment. The goal is awareness and attention, not a guaranteed positive emotional state; discomfort during practice does not mean the practice failed.
  • Distinct techniques, not interchangeable: breathing technique (attention anchored to breath), body-scan relaxation (systematic attention to body regions), gratitude journaling (a separate written practice), mindful walking (slow, attentive movement; walking as fast as possible works against the practice, since speed undercuts present-moment bodily attention).

Fitness components

CategoryComponents
Health-relatedBody composition, cardiovascular fitness, flexibility, muscular fitness (strength/endurance)
Skill-relatedSpeed, agility, balance, coordination, reaction time, power
  • Classification items test the reasoning, not just the label: coordination is the simultaneous, timed control of multiple body parts; balance is maintaining a stable position; speed is the rate of movement in one direction. Confusing these three is the most common distractor pattern.

Passive vs. active recreation

  • Passive: minimal physical exertion (reading, watching, listening). Active: involves physical movement or exertion. This distinction is about exertion, not about being alone versus in a group, and not about which type is inherently "better" for emotional versus physical well-being; both can support well-being through different mechanisms.

Wellness program design: the flawed-conclusion pattern

  • Consistency of participation alone does not prove a routine is well-designed if other domains (sleep, rest, recovery) are being sacrificed.
  • A single-behavior change (only exercise, say) is incomplete, because physiological development depends on interconnected patterns, not one factor in isolation.
  • A wellness log's value depends on honest, accurate self-report; a log padded to look complete removes the log's value as evidence for reflection and adjustment: a validity problem, not a cosmetic one.
  • Matching an activity to one's real time and context constraints (a shorter, sustainable practice over a longer one that gets abandoned) is a legitimate design choice, not a shortcut to be penalized.

Item design pattern

Most items present a plausible student or teacher conclusion and ask for an evaluation. The correct answer almost never simply agrees or flatly disagrees; it names the specific incompleteness (a missing domain, a misclassified component, an unexamined red flag) that the confident-sounding conclusion glosses over.

Practice Items: Lesson 3

P1. Which of the following is a skill-related, not health-related, component of fitness?

  • A. Muscular endurance
  • B. Flexibility
  • C. Agility
  • D. Cardiovascular fitness

Rationale: Agility is a skill-related component; the other three are health-related.

P2. During a relay race, which athlete is MOST directly demonstrating power rather than another skill-related component?

  • A. The sprinter accelerating explosively out of the starting blocks
  • B. The teammate maintaining balance while passing the baton
  • C. The runner adjusting pace based on the opponent's position
  • D. The athlete reacting quickly to the starting gun

Rationale: Power is the ability to exert force quickly (explosive acceleration); the other options primarily demonstrate balance, agility/decision-timing, and reaction time respectively.

P3. A student writes down three things she is grateful for each night before sleeping. Which mindfulness-adjacent practice is she using?

  • A. Body-scan relaxation
  • B. Breathing technique
  • C. Gratitude journaling
  • D. Passive recreation

Rationale: This is gratitude journaling, a distinct practice from breathing or body-scan techniques.

P4. Which statement BEST distinguishes passive from active recreation?

  • A. Passive recreation cannot improve well-being
  • B. Passive recreation involves minimal physical exertion, while active recreation involves physical movement or exertion
  • C. Active recreation must always involve a team
  • D. Passive recreation is only valuable for introverted individuals

Rationale: The distinction is exertion-based, not about group size, personality, or which type is more valuable overall.

P5. A student exercises daily and eats a balanced diet but consistently sleeps five hours a night and reports constant fatigue. Which evaluation is MOST appropriate?

  • A. Her routine is sufficient because exercise and diet are the two most important wellness factors
  • B. Her routine may be incomplete because wellness depends on interconnected patterns, including sleep, not diet and exercise alone
  • C. Her routine is ineffective because exercise cannot coexist with poor sleep
  • D. Sleep has no measurable effect on physiological development

Rationale: Physiological development depends on interconnected health-related patterns; consistent effort in two domains does not offset a third domain being neglected.

P6. A student logs "30 minutes of exercise" every day for a month, including days she skipped, because she wants a complete-looking tracker. Why does this undermine the purpose of a wellness log?

  • A. It makes the tracker visually inconsistent
  • B. It removes the log's value as accurate evidence for reflecting on and adjusting the routine
  • C. It only matters if a teacher checks it
  • D. It has no effect, since exercise itself is still healthy on the days it happened

Rationale: A log's purpose is honest evidence for reflection; padding it with unearned entries removes exactly that value, regardless of whether anyone checks it.

True/False: Lesson 3

P7. The goal of mindfulness practice is to eliminate negative emotions entirely.

False

Rationale: The goal is awareness and attention, not the elimination of any particular emotional state.

P8. Balance and coordination are both skill-related fitness components, but they are tested differently: balance concerns maintaining a stable position, and coordination concerns the simultaneous, timed control of multiple body parts.

True

Rationale: This is the precise distinction the exam's classification items rely on.

P9. A wellness plan that changes only one health behavior while leaving all others unaddressed can still be considered complete, since physiological development depends primarily on that one behavior.

False

Rationale: Physiological development depends on interconnected patterns; a single-behavior plan is incomplete by definition here.

P10. Walking briskly at the fastest sustainable pace, with full attention on each step, best fulfills the definition of mindful walking used in this course.

False

Rationale: Mindful walking is defined by attentive awareness, not by maximizing speed; the exam explicitly marks the walking-quickly claim false.
