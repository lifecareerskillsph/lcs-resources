Two frameworks before the routines

Wellness is broader than physical fitness alone. It spans physical, emotional, social, intellectual, occupational, and spiritual dimensions, someone can be physically active and still need support elsewhere.

Fitness content in this lesson is organized around five health-related fitness components: cardiovascular endurance (heart and lungs sustaining activity over time), muscular strength (maximum force in one effort), muscular endurance (sustained repeated effort), flexibility (range of motion at a joint), and body composition (the ratio of fat mass to lean mass).

Sources: Department of Education K to 12 Physical Education Curriculum Guide.

Learn

3.1 What makes an activity "fitness"?

A fitness activity is structured, outcome-directed, and repeatable: there is a planned sequence, it is selected to build or maintain a fitness component, and it can be practiced consistently over time. In this lesson, the routine every student should be able to lead without help is the dynamic warm-up.

An average of 60 minutes per day of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity across the week provides measurable health benefits for adolescents (WHO, 2020). Physical activity interventions are associated with reductions in depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Recchia et al., 2023), and the specific type of activity chosen shapes the mental-health benefit, not only whether someone is active at all (Lenze et al., 2025).

Practice

The dynamic warm-up

A dynamic warm-up uses controlled, active movement to raise heart rate and muscle temperature before activity. Static stretching belongs after activity instead, once the body is already warm. Run each movement for about 30 to 40 seconds with a short transition.

  1. March in place. Lift your knees comfortably, swing your arms naturally, keep your shoulders relaxed.
  2. Arm circles. Small circles forward, then reverse, controlled, not fast.
  3. Torso twists. Feet shoulder-width apart, knees soft, let your arms swing naturally with the twist.
  4. Bodyweight squats. Sit back like reaching for a chair, knees tracking over the toes, stand with control.
  5. Walking lunges. Step forward, both knees controlled, alternate sides.
  6. High knees. Quick steps, knees lifting comfortably, land softly.
  7. Standing hip circles. Hands on hips, slow circles both directions, this is mobility, not a stretch.
Smaller range of motion, a slower pace, a seated option, or skipping a movement entirely are all reasonable adaptations. State this before starting so no one has to ask.
Facilitator noteDemonstrate the sequence once, then lead the class through it together. Assign one student as form-checker per the activity below.
Activity 3.1: assign one leader and one form-checker. Run the seven movements in order using brief verbal cues, about 30 seconds each with short transitions. Afterward, name what changed in your heart rate, breathing, or muscle warmth.

Practice

Practice Test 3.1: Ronnie's fifteen minutes

Scenario: Ronnie wants to start exercising but has no equipment and only 15 minutes before his online class. He is unsure whether to stretch first or move first.
1. Which routine should Ronnie do first?
2. Which health-related fitness component does the warm-up NOT directly build?
3. Which dimension of wellness, beyond the physical, could a consistent movement routine support?
See the answer key

1. B — Active movement raises heart rate and prepares muscles for exertion. Static stretching is more appropriate after activity, once the body is already warm.

2. B — The warm-up improves readiness, mobility, and heart-rate response, but does not load muscles enough to build strength or endurance on its own.

3. A — Emotional wellness is the clearest answer, since physical activity is associated with lower depressive symptoms in adolescents. Social wellness is also reasonable in a shared-activity context.

Filipino context: movement and rest

Culture can make wellness routines easier, harder, or simply different. A cultural factor is not automatically protective or risky, its effect depends on the situation.

See the assets and tensions

Assets: shared movement traditions such as basketball, arnis, and folk dance; family and barkada presence that can make activity social rather than solitary; community and school spaces that support low-cost movement.

Tensions: academic workload and family expectations that can reduce time for recovery; gender-role expectations that can shape which activities feel acceptable; hiya, which may make visible breathing practice or leading a group feel uncomfortable.

Sources: Enriquez (1992); Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino (2000).

3.2 Breathing as a regulation skill

Unlike fitness activities, breathing practices intentionally slow the body down. A racing heart, shallow breathing, or a feeling of being "too activated" are signals regulation may help: control the breath with a slower, structured pattern, and a slower exhale supports a shift away from alert activation toward a calmer state.

Diaphragmatic breathing with children and adolescents is associated with reductions in physiological and psychological stress markers (Tsakona et al., 2025). A five-minute slow-breathing practice, delivered three times a week, was feasible and well tolerated by high school students with encouraging early anxiety findings (Bentley et al., 2022), and slow, diaphragmatic techniques are more broadly linked with reduced stress markers and greater parasympathetic activity (Bentley et al., 2023).

Facilitator noteThis is the centerpiece activity for 3.2. Lead the whole class through all three techniques below in order, then ask for a show of hands on which felt most natural before moving to the checkpoint.

Learn

Three breathing techniques

Learn all three. Which one you keep using is a personal choice worth naming in your Wellness Tracker.

Diaphragmatic breathing (1–2 minutes): sit comfortably, one hand on the chest and one on the belly. Inhale slowly through the nose for 4 counts, letting the belly rise. Exhale slowly for 6 counts, letting the belly fall. Repeat 6 to 8 times.

Box breathing (1–2 minutes): inhale for 4 counts, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. That is one round. Repeat for about 4 rounds.

4-7-8 breathing (about 1 minute): inhale quietly through the nose for 4 counts, hold for 7, exhale completely through the mouth for 8. Repeat only 3 to 4 rounds, this technique is stronger than the other two.

Practice

Comfort, choice, and fit

Eyes can stay open, focus on a fixed point instead of closing them. Return to normal breathing anytime you feel dizzy or uncomfortable. Breath holds are optional, skip them if you prefer.

Before a two-minute window like a quiz, box breathing or a few rounds of 4-7-8 give a clear count to follow. After something slower, like a stressful commute, diaphragmatic breathing works well without any breath holds at all.

Try one technique now. Which one felt most natural, and in what situation would you actually use it?

Practice

Practice Test 3.2: Anna's two minutes

Scenario: Before a long quiz, Anna notices her heart racing and her thoughts moving too fast to focus. She has two minutes before the quiz starts.
1. Which breathing technique would be most efficient for Anna?
2. What adaptation can she use if she does not want to close her eyes in front of classmates?
3. Why can a slower exhale help calm the body?
See the answer key

1. A — Box breathing or 4-7-8 breathing are both brief and structured. Diaphragmatic breathing can also work, but may take slightly longer to settle into.

2. A — Keeping the eyes open and breathing quietly does not require closing the eyes for the technique to work.

3. A — Slowing the exhale helps shift the nervous system away from high alert and toward greater regulation.
